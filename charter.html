Reflection — What I Learned from the Team Charter

Before GoShuttle, I thought a team charter was simply a document you fill in at the start of a project and then forget about. My experience in Professional Practice 3 changed that view completely. The charter became a living reference that we returned to whenever there was disagreement or confusion — particularly around the strict package ordering rule, which we had to enforce in mid-August when the team was tempted to start Package 3 (LiveTrip) before Package 2 (Routes & Stops) was fully seeded.

As Team Leader, co-creating the charter rather than writing it alone taught me the difference between rules that are imposed and rules that are owned. When the team co-wrote the working agreements, they respected them. The "package ordering is strict" rule, for example, was Mvuyisi's suggestion — so he was the first to enforce it on himself when he had to wait for Sinethemba's Route table before he could create LiveTrip foreign keys.

The core values section was the hardest part to write meaningfully. It is easy to write "Collaboration" as a value, but the charter forced us to define what collaboration actually looks like in practice — which led to the WhatsApp standup rule and the Trello-before-coding agreement. These operational definitions turned abstract values into daily behaviours, which is something I will carry into every future project, including my own business projects.

If I could improve our charter, I would add a conflict resolution process. When the hosting constraint forced our architecture pivot from Spring Boot to Supabase in early August, the decision happened informally in a WhatsApp chat. A formal escalation path in our charter would have made that decision more structured and better documented.
