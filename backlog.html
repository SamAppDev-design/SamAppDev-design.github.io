Reflection — What I Learned from Managing the Product Backlog

Managing the GoShuttle product backlog taught me that prioritisation is a skill, not just a sorting exercise. In the beginning I listed everything I thought GoShuttle should eventually do — including push notifications and real fare APIs — and marked them all as high priority. The backlog was overwhelming and the team did not know where to start.

The turning point was when I applied the principle that the backlog should be ordered by what unblocks the rest of the team. PB-02 (Campus table) and PB-03 (User table) had to be done first not because they were the most impressive features, but because every other package needed them as foreign keys. This "dependency-first" thinking became our ordering logic, and it is something that directly maps to the Agile concept of a "ready" backlog item — one that has no unresolved dependencies.

I also learned the difference between a product backlog and a wishlist. Items like PB-23 (real fare API) and PB-24 (push notifications) are legitimate future goals, but marking them as "Future" sprint items rather than "Sprint 3" kept the team focused on what could actually be built and tested within the module timeframe. A backlog that is honest about scope builds team confidence; one that is overloaded creates anxiety.

Story points were new to me in this project. Estimating effort in points rather than hours felt strange at first, but it helped us compare tasks relatively — PB-14 (TrackView.vue with Leaflet) at 6 points clearly needed more time than PB-02 (Campus table) at 2 points, without us having to commit to a specific number of hours. I will use story points on all future Agile projects.
